Journal 01 / COS650 capstone

Building a research baseline for Yggdrasil

By Hakim Corner · November 24, 2026

This month taught me that building an intelligent-looking system and scientifically demonstrating intelligence are two very different things.

My journey and goals

My background is in IT and cybersecurity, and I'm currently pursuing my master's degree in Computer Science at Full Sail University. I've always enjoyed building software, especially AI and game systems. Over time, I became more interested in the science behind the technology. I want to understand what it takes for an artificial individual to learn from its experiences, form memories, and develop new capabilities rather than relying entirely on scripted behavior.

That interest led me to Project Yggdrasil. My long-term vision is an artificial civilization with individuals called Gahallians, whose knowledge and societies develop through experience. For the capstone, though, I'm starting with a much smaller and testable question: whether one individual can discover an unfamiliar material transformation and reproduce it later.

What I developed this month

The biggest technical addition was a research-baseline runner in the existing TypeScript engine. I used Node.js, Vitest, and the project's simulation infrastructure to execute four configurations with fixed seeds, compare fingerprints of the resulting states, and export the measurements as JSON and CSV. The Electron, React, and three.js desktop lab remains my research observatory. The November release, v0.3.8, adds verification and documentation rather than a new learning system in the desktop application.

The runner makes it possible to check whether an identical starting configuration produces the same recorded result. I used the following command to run the baseline:

pnpm --filter @yggdrasil/engine baseline

All four configurations passed, and the November 23 rerun reproduced the same fingerprints. The published Windows evidence also records 225 passing mind/world boundary tests and 48 passing determinism and record/replay tests. This is important because a Gahallian should learn through its own observations, not receive hidden world rules. Hosted-model replay is not implemented yet, so the deterministic conclusion does not extend to live language-model calls.

While planning the next experiment, I also looked at Voyager, particularly its approach to reusable skills and feedback. It provides a useful example of experience-based agent improvement, but Yggdrasil's proposed research requires a world with invented material properties so that the agent cannot simply rely on familiar recipes.

Retrospective: what went right

Having reproducible baseline results was the most important achievement this month. I now have a documented starting point, versioned source code, a Windows build, and raw evidence that can be used to compare future experiments. The boundary tests also gave me a way to verify the separation between the mind and the environment. Earlier coursework in software development, machine learning, and research methodology helped me understand why controls and reliable measurements matter.

Retrospective: what went wrong

Some of my earlier assumptions did not hold up when I examined the data. In stored hosted-model runs, roughly 97–98% of recorded utterances were engine-mediated belief transfers. That is not the same as one Gahallian understanding another. I also found a throughput regression at larger populations, and the full desktop acceptance run passed only eight of twelve checks. A database process-safety test still fails in Linux GitHub Actions even though the local Windows database suite passed.

These were setbacks, but they helped me realize that I need to prioritize measurement and understanding over adding more systems. I don't want to mistake an interesting simulation for evidence of capabilities that I haven't tested.

How I will improve moving forward

Feedback on my proposal highlighted the need for a more specific hypothesis, a detailed evaluation methodology, and clearer technical implementation. I revised the proposal to include a proposed 20-percentage-point improvement in successful discovery and delayed reproduction compared with a learning-disabled control, along with a separate proposed human evaluation. These are research plans, not achieved results; any human study must receive the required institutional review before recruitment.

My next step is to build the unfamiliar-material world, isolate the learning mechanism, and preregister success criteria before running the comparison. I also need to profile the performance regression, investigate the Linux CI failure, and improve the consistency of my evidence collection. I want to spend less time on the visualization and more time on experiments that answer the research question.

Overall, this month changed how I approach Yggdrasil. I still have the same long-term vision, but I now understand that the best way to get there is by establishing one measurable capability at a time. I'm ready to move into the first controlled discovery experiment once its design and acceptance criteria are finalized.
